Research context

Using analytics to explore an agrifood transition problem

Nature-inclusive agriculture is not only an ecological practice choice. It is embedded in farmer capabilities, financial conditions, perceived risk, policy settings, social norms, value-chain relationships, market rewards and longer-term ecological feedbacks. Dutch transition research identifies interconnected barriers and lock-ins rather than a single missing incentive [1][3][6].

This exploratory project combines a project dataset of 120 farmer observations with Power BI and a structured literature base of 100 DOI-linked publications. The dashboard is used to generate hypotheses, not population estimates. The strongest simulated adoption difference is associated with training access; subsidy access shows a smaller positive difference; market-premium access is weak; perceived risk is negatively associated with adoption intention; non-adopters show greater financial pressure; and adopters show higher simulated soil-health and biodiversity scores.

Research framing

Central research question

How do interacting economic, behavioural, institutional, value-chain and ecological mechanisms shape farmers’ adoption of nature-inclusive agricultural practices?

This question builds on research showing that sustainable-practice adoption is shaped by behavioural, economic, institutional and social factors [9][10][16][17][25][55][63], while sustainability-transition scholarship emphasises regimes, pathways, governance and multi-level interactions [24][68][69][71][76][78][79].

Literature synthesis

A 100-paper evidence base

1. Nature-inclusive agriculture and Dutch transition dynamics

Core Dutch studies frame nature-inclusive agriculture as a transformation challenge involving economic incentives, farmer action perspectives, knowledge, governance, cultural norms, opportunity costs and regime resistance [1][3][4][5][6][7]. This justifies treating adoption as embedded in a wider agrifood system rather than as an isolated farmer preference.

2. Adoption is multi-dimensional

Reviews and meta-analyses across sustainable agriculture, conservation agriculture, agri-environmental schemes, organic farming and ecological practices repeatedly identify interacting economic, behavioural, informational, farm-level and institutional determinants [9][10][11][13][14][15][16][17][18][23][25][27][42][45][50][57][58][59][63].

3. Incentives and economic viability

Financial mechanisms can alter transition feasibility, but adoption cannot be reduced to payment size alone. Opportunity costs, incentive design, ecosystem-service payments, voluntary versus mandatory policy and long-term business viability are all relevant [6][7][10][17][37][38][39][43][47].

4. Behaviour, identity and social influence

Farmers' choices are also social and psychological. Good-farmer identity, attitudes, trust, peer effects, social norms, self-identity and social capital can influence environmental management and adoption trajectories [5][12][26][35][36][40][41][44][46][54][55][56][60][61][62][65].

5. Learning, training and information

Knowledge systems, extension, information quality, experiential learning, farmer-to-farmer learning and digital capabilities can affect how farmers evaluate innovations and uncertainty [22][37][48][52][53][59][64][66][67].

6. Ecological outcomes and ecosystem services

Biodiversity, ecosystem services and soil health provide the ecological outcome side of the transition. The literature supports examining whether changes in practice are associated with ecological functions, arthropod abundance, soil condition, soil carbon and multifunctionality [8][28][29][30][31][32][43][44][82][84][96][97][98][99][100].

7. From determinants to transition systems

Transition research moves the analysis from static determinants to interactions among niches, regimes, institutions, governance and scaling processes [24][68][69][70][71][72][73][74][75][76][77][78][79][80][81][83][85][86][87].

8. Systems modelling and participation

Participatory modelling, group model building, agent-based modelling and system dynamics offer methods for representing feedback, stakeholder assumptions, scenario behaviour and transition pathways [88][89][90][91][92][93][94][95]. These methods are particularly relevant for converting the exploratory dashboard into a causal-loop and simulation research programme.

Systems interpretation

Hypothesised feedback structure

The dashboard does not establish causality. Instead, it helps formulate relationships that can later be tested through interviews, workshops, causal-loop diagrams and simulation.

R1 Learning loop: Training ↑ → Knowledge ↑ → Perceived risk ↓ → Adoption intention ↑ → Adoption ↑ → Practical experience ↑ → Knowledge ↑ B1 Financial constraint loop: Financial pressure ↑ → Perceived transition risk ↑ → Adoption intention ↓ → Adoption ↓ R2 Social diffusion loop: Visible adoption ↑ → Peer influence / social legitimacy ↑ → Adoption intention ↑ → Adoption ↑ R3 Ecological-benefit loop: Adoption ↑ → Soil health / biodiversity ↑ → Perceived long-term benefits ↑ → Adoption attractiveness ↑

These hypothesised loops draw conceptually on adoption, social-norm, transition and systems-modelling literatures [16][40][55][68][71][74][88][89][92][94][95].

Dataset: A 120-observation project dataset was developed for the analysis, covering adoption, training, subsidies, market access, perceived risk, financial pressure, value-chain coordination, policy support, soil health and biodiversity indicators.

Method

Exploratory analytical design

The dataset contains 120 synthetic farmer records with variables for farm characteristics, nature-inclusive adoption, training, subsidy access, market-premium access, perceived risk, peer influence, buyer pressure, biodiversity awareness, expected profitability, financial pressure, digital literacy, soil health, biodiversity, income, investment, value-chain coordination, policy support and adoption intention.

Power BI is used as an exploratory visual-analytics layer to identify descriptive patterns. The literature base is then used to interpret whether the relationships represented in the prototype are theoretically plausible and to identify mechanisms requiring empirical validation.
